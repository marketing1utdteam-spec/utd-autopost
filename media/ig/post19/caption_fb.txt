Furniture and décor might be the hardest honest category in ecommerce: high prices, bulky shipping, and a product whose entire value is how it feels in a room — sold through a six-inch screen. Generic themes treat a sofa like a t-shirt: thumbnail, price, button. This carousel is the list of what the niche actually requires.

Five needs, one per slide: rooms, not thumbnails — décor is sold by atmosphere, full-width room scenes instead of product-on-white grids; shop-the-look — one room photo where every item is tappable, the highest-intent purchase path in the niche; materials up close — zoomable texture that replaces the showroom hand-touch; delivery answered early — bulky-item shipping anxiety kills orders at checkout unless costs and timelines are visible upfront; and reviews with photos — real customers' interiors are the strongest proof a décor store can show.

The data behind each need: Salsify's consumer research puts product content at "decisive" for 87% of shoppers — in décor, that content is texture and context, not specs. Baymard's checkout studies show unexpected costs are the #1 abandonment reason (48%) — for furniture, that's almost always shipping, which is why slide four exists. And photo reviews consistently lift conversion in Bazaarvoice's network data, with décor among the strongest categories, because a couch in a stranger's real living room is proof no studio shot can fake.

Article (the Canadian DTC furniture brand) built a nine-figure business on exactly these mechanics: oversized room photography, transparent delivery pricing displayed before checkout, and customer-photo galleries woven into product pages. Their product pages read like interior magazines with a buy button — and the company became one of North America's fastest-growing furniture retailers before its acquisition, outconverting legacy players with 10× their catalog. The container did the differentiating; the furniture was comparable.

My take: in décor, the theme isn't presentation — it's the substitute for your showroom.
